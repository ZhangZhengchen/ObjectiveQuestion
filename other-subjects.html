LIT01Single choice

Narrative perspective and informational access

A narrator may speak in the first or third person, and the chosen perspective shapes what readers can know about events and characters. An omniscient narrator may reveal multiple characters’ actions, thoughts, and feelings, whereas a first-person account raises questions about the narrator’s knowledge and reliability.

Retained judgment: Select the most defensible integrated account of who narrates, what the narrator knows at different times, what remains inaccessible, and how those limits shape the reader’s understanding.

Source: Approaching prose fiction, section 2.3 · Approaching prose fiction, section 2.3

Use only this fictional passage. Which interpretation is most defensible? “I was nine when Lina tied a blue ribbon to our fence. I assumed it marked my birthday. Fifteen years later, her letter told me it warned my father to leave town. That morning, I noticed Lina glance repeatedly toward the road, though I still cannot say why. Niko saw the ribbon too, but I never discovered what he thought it meant.”

AA participating narrator remains limited to childhood knowledge; the letter adds no new understanding, leaving the reader’s view of the signal unchanged.
BOne participating narrator contrasts a childhood assumption with later knowledge; Lina’s gesture is observed, not explained, so readers revise the event while uncertainty remains.
CTwo narrators recount the event: the child knows Lina is afraid, while the adult learns that Niko misunderstood the signal.
DAn omniscient voice reports all three characters’ minds; the later letter confirms Niko’s guilt and resolves the earlier uncertainty.

Whole-item exact match; no partial credit.

Reference answer and explanation

B (One participating narrator contrasts a childhood assumption with later knowledge; Lina’s gesture is observed, not explained, so readers revise the event while uncertainty remains.)

B is supported by the first-person voice, the contrast between the childhood assumption and later discovery, and the explicit lack of access to Lina’s reason and Niko’s belief. D invents complete mental access and guilt. C mistakes temporal viewpoints for separate narrators and invents inner knowledge. A overlooks the later discovery and consequent revision.

Five-stage design record

Stage 1: assessment target and observable evidence

Determine what a narrator can and cannot know in a supplied passage and judge how that access shapes the reader’s understanding, using specific narrative details.

The judgment distinguishes the narrating voice from the characters, identifies passage-level evidence of access to or restriction from thoughts, feelings, or events, and limits conclusions to what that evidence supports.

Characterisation through represented action and speech

Characterisation comprises the strategies used to present and develop fictional characters. Writers may tell readers about a character directly or show the character externally through reported speech and behaviour, allowing readers to infer attitudes and values.

Retained judgment: Judge which analysis most defensibly infers a specific aspect of Mina's character from her represented action or speech and explains the relevance of that detail.

Source: Approaching prose fiction, section 2.5 · Approaching prose fiction, section 2.5

Characterisation can show a fictional character through speech and behaviour. At closing time, Mina notices that a new volunteer has mislabelled three food parcels. She quietly replaces the labels and says, “I’ll show you our system tomorrow. It took me a week to learn it.” When the supervisor asks who made the error, Mina replies, “They’re correct now.” Using only these details, which analysis makes the strongest character inference?

AMina is protective: by saying only that the parcels are correct, she avoids identifying the inexperienced volunteer for blame.
BMina is controlling: by correcting the labels, she prevents the volunteer from learning the system independently.
CMina is careless: her promise to explain the system tomorrow shows that she accepts incorrectly labelled parcels.
DMina values recognition: quietly replacing the labels ensures that the supervisor knows she solved the problem.

Whole-item exact match; no partial credit.

Reference answer and explanation

A (Mina is protective: by saying only that the parcels are correct, she avoids identifying the inexperienced volunteer for blame.)

A makes the strongest accurate connection between a specific character inference and Mina’s represented speech. Her answer redirects attention from who erred to the corrected parcels, supporting a protective reading. B is possible but overstates what correcting the labels proves. C conflicts with her correction of the parcels. D conflicts with the action being quiet and provides no evidence that Mina seeks recognition.

Sequence of events in narrative order

A narrative consists not only of events but also of the order in which those events are presented. Summarising a narrative therefore requires identifying crucial events and tracking their arrangement, while recognising that readers may differ about which events are key.

Retained judgment: Judge independently whether three claims about the presence or presented arrangement of crucial narrative events are source-supported, without adding unstated events or chronology.

Source: Approaching prose fiction, section 2.2 · Approaching prose fiction, section 2.2

Read the micro-story. Jonah found a brass key inside an old coat. At noon, he carried it to the railway station. An attendant used it to open a suitcase marked with Jonah’s name but offered no explanation. That evening, Jonah boarded a train without the suitcase. For each claim, choose Source-supported if it is stated or directly established; otherwise choose Not source-supported. Judge presented order—the order in which the story tells events—and do not add plausible events or chronology.

c1Jonah finds the brass key before going to the station.
c2The attendant tells Jonah why his name is written on the suitcase.
c3The suitcase is opened before Jonah departs on the evening train.

Whole-item exact match; no partial credit.

Reference answer and explanation

c1: Supported; c2: Not supported; c3: Supported

Claim 1 is supported because finding the key is presented before the station visit. Claim 2 is unsupported: the attendant opens the suitcase but gives no explanation of the name. Claim 3 is supported because the opening occurs before Jonah boards the train.

Five-stage design record

Stage 1: assessment target and observable evidence

Judge source-supported claims about the arrangement of crucial narrative events without adding unstated events or chronology.

Record several event-arrangement judgments independently for a short invented passage. Each claim concerns the sequence or presence of a crucial event; accepting one complete summary does not reveal which other claims the learner accepts.

Stage 2: scenario and open task

Justify every judgment with passage details. You may identify a different event as crucial if you explain why. Do not add events or chronology.

Narrative arrangement and reader expectation

Writers arrange narrative events to encourage readers to continue and discover what happens next. Openings may raise questions or establish expectations, although readers can differ in what they find compelling and a brief opening provides limited grounds for judging the whole narrative.

Retained judgment: Choose the editorial judgment that identifies a specific unresolved question or expectation, connects the ordered information to interest in what follows, and remains limited to what the opening establishes.

Source: Approaching prose fiction, section 2.2 · Approaching prose fiction, section 2.2

A student magazine is considering this opening for its fiction page: “Mara found her own name on tomorrow’s passenger list. Beside it, someone had written ‘do not board.’ The station clock stopped as she looked up, but every traveller kept moving.” Which editorial judgment best explains how the presented information encourages interest while staying within what this brief opening establishes?

AChoose it: Mara’s discovery shows she will board despite the warning, so readers can anticipate the complete plot with confidence.
BDecline it: the clock detail may seem intriguing, but personal taste alone determines whether the opening creates any unresolved expectation.
CDecline it: the stopped clock proves that time travel will resolve the warning, making the whole story predictable.
DChoose it: the list first raises why Mara is named for tomorrow, and the warning then intensifies uncertainty about boarding; this may encourage reading on, though the opening alone cannot establish the whole story’s effectiveness.

Whole-item exact match; no partial credit.

Reference answer and explanation

D (Choose it: the list first raises why Mara is named for tomorrow, and the warning then intensifies uncertainty about boarding; this may encourage reading on, though the opening alone cannot establish the whole story’s effectiveness.)

D identifies a text-grounded unresolved question, explains how the ordered details intensify uncertainty, connects that effect to continued reading, and limits the claim to the opening. C and A invent later developments or resolutions. B notes a possible reader response but wrongly treats personal taste as replacing analysis of the unresolved expectation created by the passage.

Five-stage design record

Stage 1: assessment target and observable evidence

Identify a specific unresolved question or expectation created by the arrangement of information in a supplied opening and judge how the passage encourages interest in what follows.

The judgment identifies the textual information that creates the question or expectation, explains its passage-level effect, and does not assert a later resolution or overall effectiveness that the supplied text cannot establish.

Stage 2: scenario and open task

A student magazine is choosing whether this original opening should lead its fiction page: “Mara found her own name on tomorrow’s passenger list. Beside it, someone had written ‘do not board.’ The station clock stopped as she looked up, but every traveller kept moving.”

Explain how two specific details encourage—or fail to encourage—you to continue reading. Then propose one rearrangement of the existing sentences and explain how it would alter reader expectation. Do not invent a later resolution or judge the whole story from this opening.

Goal: Recommend an evidence-responsive next intervention.

Role: Research partner

Audience: The classroom teacher

Situation: The teacher must revise practice after analysing the first intervention.

Product: A brief justified recommendation

Success criteria

  • States a defensible judgment on the proposed revision
  • Links specific findings to retained, changed, or further-investigated features
  • Does not present unsupported changes as evidence-led

Stage 5: response design

Single choice best observes the focal course-grounded judgment without requiring independent production. Alternatives will differ by evidential warrant rather than by prescribing one uniquely acceptable teaching technique.

Performance not retained

  • The learner does not independently compose or explain a recommendation.
  • The learner does not independently generate a next intervention.
  • Coherent sequencing across all action-research stages and continuation into another full data-analysis cycle are not directly assessed.
